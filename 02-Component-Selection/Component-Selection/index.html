    Component Selection

    RAS 304 – Individual Datasheet (Kaushik) – Driver Nano 2: Linear Actuator A + Pressure Sensor Project: Single Robotic Spider Leg

    Scope: this page covers only my individual board (Driver Nano 2). Team-level decisions live in the team repository. Prices were checked on 2026-10-19 and will change.

    1. Subsystem summary

    Driver Nano 2 receives a target position for Linear Actuator A from the Main Processor Nano over the team bus, drives the actuator with PWM and direction signals, reads the actuator's position feedback and a foot pressure sensor, decides whether contact or a force threshold has been reached, and reports status and pressure back to the Main Processor.

    Project constraints that drive the selections:

    • Through-hole parts only (no daughter boards), PIC18F57Q43 Curiosity Nano as the MCU.
    • External 9 V barrel-jack supply, on-board 5 V linear regulator, fuse and power indicator.
    • The Curiosity Nano runs its I/O and ADC at its target voltage, which is 3.3 V as shipped (Curiosity Nano user guide, section 3.3.1). Every analog signal into the MCU must stay at or below 3.3 V.
    • 2x4 IDC bus connector to the rest of the leg.

    Assumptions to confirm with the team (they change the actuator and driver choices if they move):

    • Actuator A needs about 20 mm of stroke and no more than about 40 N at the foot.
    • The foot sensor is used for contact and threshold detection (per the software flow), not for calibrated force measurement.

    Block diagram (text form)

    9V barrel jack -> fuse -> 9V rail --+--------------------> SN754410 VCC2 -> Linear Actuator A (PQ12-P)
                                    |
                                    +-> L7805CV -> 5V rail -> Curiosity Nano (VBUS) -> 3.3V (VTG)
                                                                  |                       |
                         PWM + 2 direction pins ------------------+                       +-> actuator pot reference
                         SN754410 logic supply (5V)                                       +-> MCP6002 supply
                         2 ADC inputs <- actuator pot wiper, FSR 402 via MCP6002
                         I2C / UART <-> 2x4 IDC team bus

    Pin table (2x4 IDC team bus) – DRAFT, must match the team block diagram

    MCU resources needed on my board (pin mapping is done in the schematic assignment): 1 PWM output, 2 digital outputs (direction), 2 ADC inputs, the bus peripheral pins, 1 digital input (stop line).

    Labels used below: [Actuator A subsystem], [Pressure sensor subsystem], [Power subsystem].

    2. Major component selections

    2.1 [Actuator A subsystem] Linear actuator with position feedback

    Solution Pros Cons
    Option 1. Actuonix PQ12-P, 20 mm, 100:1, 6 V, potentiometer feedback

    $81.76 each
    RobotShop product page · PQ12 datasheet    		 • Rated 6 V, which matches what the motor sees from a 9 V rail after the bipolar driver's saturation drop (see choice rationale)
    • Built-in 5 kΩ position potentiometer gives closed-loop feedback with one ADC pin and no extra sensor
    • Peak power point of 40 N at 6 mm/s, 10 mm/s no load, enough for a leg joint assumption of about 40 N
    • Stall current of only 550 mA at 6 V keeps the supply and driver small
    • Compact 20 mm stroke body, easy to mount on a printed leg    		 • 20% maximum duty cycle in the datasheet, so firmware must limit continuous driving
    • Potentiometer is 5 kΩ ±50%, so each unit needs calibration
    • Datasheet gives no input voltage tolerance, so firmware must cap PWM duty
    • Expensive for a student project
    • Only 20 mm of stroke limits leg range
    Option 2. Actuonix PQ12-P, 20 mm, 100:1, 12 V, potentiometer feedback

    $81.76 each
    RobotShop product page · PQ12 datasheet    		 • Same body, feedback pot and mounting as Option 1
    • Low stall current of 210 mA at 12 V, easy on the supply
    • Peak power point of 40 N at 6 mm/s at its rated 12 V    		 • Needs 12 V, but the project supply is 9 V and the driver drops about 2.5 to 3.5 V, so the motor would see only about 6 V, half its rating, and lose speed and force
    • Adding a boost converter would break the single 9 V and 5 V rail plan
    • Same 20% duty cycle and pot tolerance limits as Option 1
    • Same price as the 6 V version with no benefit at 9 V
    Option 3. Actuonix L12-P, 50 mm, 100:1, 12 V, analog position feedback

    $100.63 each
    RobotShop product page (datasheet linked from the listing)    		 • 50 mm of stroke gives more leg travel
    • Fast: 23 mm/s no load
    • Low 200 mA stall current at 12 V
    • Analog position output suits the ADC    		 • 12 V rating has the same under-voltage problem as Option 2
    • Peak power point is only 23 N at 6 mm/s, lower than the PQ12
    • Largest and most expensive option, harder to fit on a spider leg
    • Longer body adds mass at the joint
    Option 4 (custom). DC gearmotor + lead screw + linear potentiometer, built in-house
    Not a commercial part, no single product link or unit price    		 • Fully tunable stroke, speed and force
    • Could run directly at 6 V to 9 V
    • Parts are individually replaceable    		 • Needs a custom mechanical design (screw, nut, guides, coupling), which is a large schedule risk this semester
    • Needs its own position sensing and end stops
    • Backlash and alignment problems likely
    • Total cost and size are unknown until designed

    Choice: Option 1, Actuonix PQ12-P 6 V, 100:1 [Actuator A subsystem]

    Rationale: The bipolar H-bridge I chose (section 2.2) drops roughly 2.6 to 3.6 V in total near 0.6 A (high-side 1.4 to 1.8 V plus low-side 1.2 to 1.8 V, datasheet ranges for this driver family), so a 9 V rail delivers about 5.4 to 6.4 V to the motor. That matches the 6 V rating, so the actuator reaches its rated 40 N and 10 mm/s peak-power point. The 12 V versions would run at about half their rated voltage. The built-in potentiometer lets Driver Nano 2 report position back to the Main Processor with a single ADC pin. I accept the 20% duty cycle and the pot tolerance as limits, and handle them with firmware duty-cycle limiting and a per-unit calibration. The 6 V actuator on the 9 V rail needs a bench check of the motor-terminal voltage before full-speed testing.

    2.2 [Actuator A subsystem] Motor driver (H-bridge)

    Solution Pros Cons
    Option 1. TI SN754410NE quadruple half-H driver, DIP-16

    $4.50 each
    SparkFun product page · TI datasheet    		 • 1 A continuous per channel (2 A peak), about 1.7 times the 550 to 600 mA actuator stall current
    • Built-in clamp diodes, so no external flyback diodes
    • Logic high threshold of 2 V minimum works with the Nano's 3.3 V outputs
    • 4.5 to 36 V motor supply covers the 9 V rail
    • Thermal shutdown built in
    • Through-hole DIP-16, pin-compatible with the L293D as a fallback    		 • Bipolar outputs drop about 2.6 to 3.6 V total, so efficiency is poor and the driver dissipates roughly 1.5 to 2 W at stall
    • Needs the four ground pins soldered to a copper pour for heat sinking
    • Quiescent supply current is high for a logic IC (tens of mA)
    • No built-in current sensing
    Option 2. L293D dual H-bridge, DIP-16

    $8.95 each (price as listed)
    Adafruit product page · TI datasheet    		 • Internal kickback diodes
    • 4.5 to 36 V motor supply
    • Well documented, many examples
    • Same DIP-16 footprint as Option 1    		 • Only 600 mA continuous per channel, which is no margin against a 550 to 600 mA stall
    • 1.2 A peak limit is only for 100 µs
    • Logic high threshold of 2.3 V is closer to the 3.3 V output level
    • More expensive than the SN754410 on this listing
    Option 3. ST L298N dual full bridge, Multiwatt-15

    $14.42 each
    Newark product page · ST product page and datasheet    		 • 2 A continuous per channel, large margin
    • Sense pins allow a shunt resistor for current monitoring
    • Handles up to 46 V    		 • Needs four external fast diodes for the inductive load
    • Total saturation drop up to 3.2 V at 1 A, which is large for a 9 V rail
    • High quiescent current on both supplies (tens of mA)
    • Bulky vertical Multiwatt package and the most expensive option
    • Overkill for a 550 mA actuator
    Option 4 (custom). Discrete MOSFET H-bridge (logic-level N-FETs and P-FETs with gate drive)
    Not a single commercial part, no unit price    		 • Very low voltage drop, so the motor sees nearly the full 9 V and less heat is dissipated
    • Scales to higher current later    		 • Needs shoot-through protection and level-shifted gate drive for the high side
    • Many more parts and board area
    • Higher design and debug risk on a first board
    • With almost no drop, a 6 V motor would see nearly the full 9 V, so the PWM duty would have to be capped tightly to avoid overvoltage

    Choice: Option 1, SN754410NE [Actuator A subsystem]

    Rationale: It is the only through-hole driver here that gives real current margin (1 A continuous against about 0.6 A stall) without external diodes or heavy packaging. Its saturation drop is the reason the 6 V actuator works from a 9 V rail. The 2 V logic threshold is compatible with the Nano's 3.3 V I/O, and it is pin-compatible with the L293D if I need a different supplier. The drawbacks (power dissipation and quiescent current) are handled by a copper pour on the ground pins, firmware duty-cycle limits, and the budget in the Power Budget page.

    2.3 [Pressure sensor subsystem] Pressure / force sensor

    Solution Pros Cons
    Option 1. Interlink FSR 402 force-sensing resistor

    $13.49 each
    Pololu product page · Interlink manufacturer page    		 • Responds from about 0.2 N to 20 N, which suits foot contact detection
    • 0.58 in diameter active area is large enough to catch a foot strike
    • Thin and flexible, easy to mount on the foot
    • Simple resistive output, so a divider and one op-amp stage is enough
    • Lower total cost than the load cell once the load cell's instrumentation amplifier is counted, and about 40% less than the FlexiForce    		 • Resistance falls from over 1 MΩ unloaded to hundreds of ohms under heavy pressure, so the front end must handle a wide range
    • Limited absolute accuracy and drift, so it suits thresholds better than calibrated force
    • Fragile tail and connector area, needs strain relief
    Option 2. Tekscan FlexiForce A201-100 (100 lb)

    $22.95 each
    SparkFun product page · Datasheet    		 • Better repeatability than a typical FSR for the same circuit
    • 0 to 100 lb range survives heavy foot loads
    • Published datasheet with a recommended op-amp circuit    		 • 100 lb range is far above the expected foot load, so light contact gives a small signal
    • About 70% more expensive than the FSR 402
    • Small sensing area at the tip can miss off-center contact
    • Still needs op-amp conditioning
    Option 3. SparkFun TAL220 10 kg bar load cell

    $12.95 each
    SparkFun product page · Datasheet    		 • Most accurate and linear of the three, suitable for real force measurement
    • 10 kg capacity with strain-gauge bridge, so stable over time
    • Inexpensive for a bridge sensor of this accuracy    		 • Millivolt-level bridge output needs a custom instrumentation amplifier with several op-amps or a dedicated IC, which adds board area and risk
    • 80 mm bar needs a proper mechanical mount and load path, awkward on a small leg foot
    • Heavy compared with a film sensor
    • Overscoped for contact detection

    Choice: Option 1, Interlink FSR 402 [Pressure sensor subsystem]

    Rationale: The software flow only asks whether contact is detected or a threshold is reached, so absolute accuracy matters less than sensitivity at low force and a simple front end. The FSR 402's 0.2 N to 20 N range matches a foot contact event, it is flat and light enough for a leg, and it has the lowest total cost once the load cell's extra amplifier circuitry is counted. The load cell would be more accurate but needs a custom instrumentation amplifier and a mount the leg does not have room for. The FlexiForce's 100 lb range is too coarse for light contact. I accept the FSR's drift and nonlinearity because the firmware compares against a threshold and can recalibrate the baseline at startup.

    2.4 [Pressure sensor subsystem] Signal-conditioning op-amp

    Solution Pros Cons
    Option 1. Microchip MCP6002-I/P dual op-amp, DIP-8

    $0.41 each
    TME product page · Datasheet    		 • Runs from 1.8 to 6 V, so it can sit on the Nano's 3.3 V rail and its output can never exceed what the ADC allows
    • Rail-to-rail input and output
    • 1 MHz bandwidth, plenty for a force signal
    • Dual package gives a spare amplifier for a second filter stage
    • Very low supply current
    • Very inexpensive    		 • Only 1 MHz, not suited to later high-speed ideas
    • Low output drive, fine for an ADC but not for loads
    • Limited to 6 V maximum supply
    Option 2. TI LM358P dual op-amp, DIP-8

    $0.28 each
    TME product page · Datasheet    		 • Lowest price and widely available
    • Input range includes ground, convenient for single-supply sensing
    • 3 to 32 V supply range is flexible    		 • Output cannot swing within about 1.5 V of its positive supply, so on 3.3 V it only reaches about 1.8 V, which wastes ADC range
    • On the 5 V rail the output can reach about 3.5 V, above the Nano's 3.3 V limit, so it would need a clamp
    • Only 0.7 MHz bandwidth for the legacy part
    • Higher quiescent current than the MCP6002
    Option 3. Microchip MCP6022-I/P dual op-amp, DIP-8

    $1.70 each
    TME product page · Datasheet (MCP6021/2/3/4 family)    		 • 2.5 to 5.5 V supply, rail-to-rail, works on 3.3 V
    • 10 MHz bandwidth leaves room for faster filtering
    • Lower noise for small signals    		 • About four times the price of the MCP6002
    • Draws more supply current
    • Bandwidth is more than a force signal needs

    Choice: Option 1, MCP6002-I/P [Pressure sensor subsystem]

    Rationale: The key constraint is that the Nano's ADC only accepts up to 3.3 V. The MCP6002 runs directly from the 3.3 V rail with rail-to-rail output, so its output physically cannot exceed the ADC range, and no clamp is needed. The LM358P cannot give a useful swing at 3.3 V, and on 5 V it could overdrive the input. The MCP6022's extra bandwidth does not help a slowly varying force signal. The spare amplifier in the package is available for a second filter stage if the FSR signal is noisy.

    2.5 [Power subsystem] 5 V linear voltage regulator

    Solution Pros Cons
    Option 1. ST L7805CV 5 V regulator, TO-220

    $0.65 each
    Jameco product page · ST datasheet    		 • Up to 1.5 A with internal current limiting and thermal shutdown
    • Specified for 7 to 18 V input, which brackets the 9 V adapter
    • Output capacitor only needed for transient response, so it is forgiving
    • Very inexpensive and easy to source
    • TO-220 takes a heatsink if needed    		 • Dropout of about 2 V at 1 A, so it needs more than 7 V in
    • Quiescent current of about 8 mA
    • Dissipates (9 V − 5 V) × load, which is about 0.9 W at my worst case
    • Thermal resistance of about 50 °C/W without a heatsink
    Option 2. TI LM2940CT-5.0 low-dropout 5 V regulator, TO-220

    $1.94 each
    Digi-Key product page · TI datasheet    		 • Dropout of about 1 V at 1 A, so it still regulates if the supply sags
    • 1 A output, up to 26 V input
    • Same TO-220 footprint as Option 1    		 • Higher quiescent current (about 15 mA)
    • More than twice the price of the L7805CV
    • Low-dropout designs are more sensitive to output capacitor choice, so the datasheet capacitor requirement must be followed
    • Dropout advantage is unused with a regulated 9 V supply

    Choice: Option 1, L7805CV [Power subsystem]

    Rationale: The input is a regulated 9 V adapter, so there is 4 V of headroom over the L7805's 2 V dropout and low dropout buys nothing. My worst-case 5 V load with margin is about 0.22 A, giving about 0.87 W dissipated and a junction rise of about 43 °C at 50 °C/W, which is acceptable without a heatsink (see the Power Budget). It is the least expensive option, forgiving on capacitors, and has built-in current limiting and thermal shutdown.

    2.6 [Power subsystem] External 9 V power source

    USB power packs are not allowed for this project, so all options are wall adapters.

    Solution Pros Cons
    Option 1. Adafruit 9 V 1 A regulated adapter, 5.5 × 2.1 mm, center-positive

    $8.95 each
    Adafruit product page    		 • Regulated output
    • Barrel size and positive tip are stated on the page
    • UL listed
    • Lowest price    		 • 1 A is not enough: my worst-case need with margin is about 1.02 A, so remaining current would be negative
    • No headroom for added sensors later
    Option 2. Pololu 9 V 1 A regulated adapter, 5.5 × 2.1 mm, center-positive

    $11.11 each
    Pololu product page    		 • Regulated, polarity stated, 1.5 m cord
    • Light, small body
    • Reliable supplier    		 • Same 1 A limit fails the budget
    • Most expensive of the three
    Option 3. SparkFun 9 V 2 A regulated adapter, 5.5 × 2.1 mm

    $10.95 each
    SparkFun product page · Datasheet    		 • 2 A leaves about 980 mA of margin after my 25% safety factor
    • Regulated output
    • Room for stall-current surges and later changes    		 • Plug polarity is not stated on the product page, so it must be checked before connecting
    • Larger body than the 1 A adapters

    Choice: Option 3, SparkFun 9 V 2 A adapter [Power subsystem]

    Rationale: The budget (see Power Budget) needs about 1.02 A from the supply including the 25% margin, which fails both 1 A adapters and passes the 2 A unit with about 0.98 A to spare. Before using it, I will check the polarity in its datasheet and with a multimeter, since the barrel jack and reverse-polarity protection assume center-positive.
